## 1. Before You Give an Agent a Key

**Suggested deck:** A practical authority map for the systems that can act on your behalf.

AI tools are no longer limited to producing a paragraph, a summary, or a rough outline. A growing category of tools can collect information, call connected services, prepare messages, update records, and take other actions on a user’s behalf. That shift is why the mundane questions matter more than the impressive demo: *What can this system see? What can it prepare? What can it actually change? And who must say yes?*

NIST’s AI Agent Standards Initiative describes a future in which agents capable of autonomous actions function securely on behalf of users. Its linked work includes questions of agent security, identity, and authorization.[1] NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework is voluntary; it is meant to help organizations incorporate trustworthiness considerations into the design, development, use, and evaluation of AI systems.[2] Neither source gives a small business a one-page certification. Both point toward a useful habit: **authority should be made visible before it is automated.**

Try a small authority map before you connect a new agent to anything important. Put one task at the top of a page—“prepare a customer follow-up,” “organize research,” or “summarize an inbox.” Then answer six questions in plain language.

| Question | Example answer for a research assistant |
|---|---|
| What may it observe? | Public web sources and a designated project folder; not private client records. |
| What may it draft? | A source-linked research brief and an email draft. |
| What may it decide? | It may rank sources; it may not make a purchasing, hiring, or publishing decision. |
| What may it send or change? | Nothing externally and no system changes. |
| Who approves the consequential step? | The named project owner. |
| What is the stop condition? | Disconnect the tool and preserve the work record if sources, permissions, or behavior are unclear. |

This exercise is intentionally less glamorous than an “autonomous workflow.” Its value is that it turns an implied hand-off into an explicit one. A system can be useful without being trusted with every action. A human can remain accountable without doing every repetitive step manually.

Begin with the lowest-risk version of the task. Let the system observe a limited set of information. Let it prepare a draft. Require a human to review the draft before anything is sent, changed, purchased, published, or represented as true. Expand authority only when the benefit is concrete and the owner can explain the boundary to another person.

The goal is not to fear AI or to delay every experiment. The goal is to avoid the most common confusion in automation: mistaking an efficient tool for an accountable actor. If you cannot name the owner, the permission, the approval point, and the emergency stop, the system has been given more authority than the team can yet govern.

**A small next step:** Choose one AI-enabled task this week and write its authority map before you connect another account, data source, or action tool.

## 2. The Forty-Five-Minute Resilience Drill

**Suggested deck:** A calm rehearsal for the day an important system stops behaving normally.

Most small teams do not need a dramatic cyber incident to discover that their response plan is vague. A lost laptop, a suspicious login notification, a locked inbox, or a payment platform outage is enough to reveal whether people know who calls whom, which account matters most, and where recovery information lives.

CISA’s small-business guidance makes an important distinction: following practical security guidance is **not a guarantee** that an organization will avoid an incident. It does, however, lay groundwork for a stronger program. CISA emphasizes leadership ownership, multifactor authentication, written incident-response plans, tabletop exercises, patching, and tested backups.[1] That is a useful model for Beacon readers because it treats preparedness as a practiced operating capability rather than a product purchase or a fear campaign.

> A resilience drill is not a test of whether your team is “good at cybersecurity.” It is a chance to make the next confusing hour less confusing.

Set aside forty-five minutes. Use a fictional scenario, such as: *A team member receives an unexpected login alert for the company email account, and a customer says they received a strange message.* Do not investigate a real incident during this exercise. The purpose is to learn where responsibility is unclear.

| Time | Exercise | Outcome to record |
|---:|---|---|
| 0–5 minutes | Name the facilitator and the fictional scenario. | Who owns the drill and what is assumed to have happened? |
| 5–15 minutes | Identify the three systems that would matter first. | Email, payments, customer records, scheduling, or another real dependency. |
| 15–30 minutes | Assign the first human actions. | Who checks the alert, who contacts support, who informs leadership, and who communicates externally if needed? |
| 30–40 minutes | Find the recovery information. | Where are admin contacts, backup status, recovery codes, vendor support paths, and escalation notes stored? |
| 40–45 minutes | Select one small repair. | A tested backup date, a missing owner, an MFA follow-up, or a clearer contact list. |

The right output is not a polished binder. It is one tested improvement that has an owner and a date. Perhaps the team learns that the recovery contacts are stored only in a locked account. Perhaps it learns that nobody knows who can reset a critical service. Perhaps it realizes that backup status is assumed rather than tested. Each is a better discovery in a quiet room than during a real disruption.

This is also a leadership exercise. CISA notes that security culture cannot be delegated entirely to IT and recommends leadership involvement in incident-response planning and tabletop exercises.[1] For a small team, that can simply mean the person with authority shows up, asks clear questions, and removes one obstacle after the drill.

**A small next step:** Schedule one forty-five-minute fictional exercise this quarter. Keep it calm, name the owner, record one improvement, and choose a date to verify that improvement.

## 3. AI Can Assist the Work. It Cannot Carry the Claim.

**Suggested deck:** A proof trail for the promises a business makes with help from AI.

AI can make it easier to organize notes, compare source material, produce a first draft, and find repetitive language. It cannot make an unsupported promise true. That distinction matters most when a tool helps create a public claim about results, earnings, reviews, professional capability, or what a customer should expect.

The Federal Trade Commission’s general business guidance says advertising claims must be truthful, not deceptive or unfair, and evidence-based.[1] Its advertising-substantiation policy explains the agency’s reasonable-basis approach to objective claims: the support should exist before the claim is disseminated.[2] Those are public regulatory materials, not a substitute for advice from qualified counsel. For a builder or small team, they still suggest a practical editorial discipline: **keep a readable proof trail before the claim leaves the room.**

The FTC’s Operation AI Comply announcement offers a useful cautionary context. It describes agency actions and allegations involving AI-marketed business opportunities, fake-review tooling, and claims about services that did not match the evidence alleged in the cases.[3] The point is not that every AI use is suspicious, or that a reader has committed a violation. The point is that the words “AI-powered” do not erase the need to show the work, the evidence, and the limits behind a public promise.

Before publishing a material claim, create a short record like this:

| Proof-trail field | Question to answer |
|---|---|
| Assertion | What exactly are we saying a product, service, or program does? |
| Evidence | What source, test, record, or firsthand information supports that assertion? |
| Limitation | What does the evidence not establish, and for whom might the result differ? |
| Reader-facing wording | Does the copy say only what the evidence can support? |
| Human reviewer | Who has checked the source, the wording, and the date? |
| Review date | When will the claim be rechecked or retired? |

This is not bureaucracy for its own sake. It makes it easier to fix a sentence before it becomes an expectation. It also makes collaboration safer: the person using an AI writing tool does not have to pretend they personally remember every source, while the accountable reviewer does not have to reconstruct the entire reasoning chain from a polished paragraph.

Avoid converting a prompt into a promise. “Draft a testimonial” is not a testimonial from a real customer. “Estimate a likely result” is not evidence that a customer will receive that result. “Explain this legal issue” is not the same as professional legal advice. If a statement is important enough to influence someone’s money, trust, health, legal position, or major decision, it deserves a human review and an evidence trail that can be read without the tool.

**A small next step:** Select one current public claim—on a website, sales page, proposal, or social post—and complete a proof-trail record before you revise or reuse it.
