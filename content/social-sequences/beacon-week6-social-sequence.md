### Tuesday — The Compact

The Constitution is not a list of things the government allows you to do.

Read that again.

It is a list of things the government is NOT allowed to do to you. The Bill of Rights does not grant you freedom of speech. It prohibits the government from taking it away. You already had it. The document simply puts the government on notice.

This distinction matters. Because the moment you believe your rights come FROM the government, you accept that the government can take them back. That is not what the document says. That is what you were taught it says.

The compact is simple: we loan power to the government. Not the other way around. And when the government stops serving the people who loaned it that power, the document provides the mechanism to correct it.

We have been trained to think of citizenship as passive. Vote every four years. Pay your taxes. Stay out of trouble. That is not citizenship. That is compliance.

Citizenship is active. It is showing up. It is knowing what your local government is doing. It is running for the school board when nobody else will. It is reading the nine pages and understanding what they actually say.

### Thursday — "The Cost of Passive Citizenship"

Every founder I know can tell you their customer acquisition cost, their monthly burn rate, and their runway to profitability. They track these numbers obsessively because they understand that what you don't measure, you can't manage.

Almost none of them can tell you who sits on their local school board. Or what their city council voted on last month. Or which planning commission decisions will affect their property values, their commute, or their children's education over the next decade.

This is not a criticism. It is a diagnosis.

We have been trained to believe that civic engagement is someone else's job. That politics is a spectator sport best observed from a distance. That the professionals will handle it.

The professionals are not handling it. 300,000 local government seats are vacant or unchallenged. The people making decisions about your community are often the only people who showed up. Not the most qualified. Not the most representative. The only ones who bothered.

The business parallel is obvious: imagine running a company where 300,000 positions sat empty because nobody applied. You would call that a crisis. You would call that a failure of leadership, of culture, of systems.

That is exactly what it is.

Personal responsibility is not a political slogan. It is the operating principle that makes self-governance possible. And self-governance — at the local level, where it actually touches your life — requires the same intentionality you bring to your business.

Beacon's manifesto says freedom and responsibility are inseparable. The evidence suggests we have been exercising the freedom while neglecting the responsibility. The seats are empty. The compact is waiting.

## Facebook — 2 Posts (Tuesday, Saturday)

### Tuesday — Long-Form Post

I want to tell you something that might sound radical, but it shouldn't be.

The Constitution of the United States is nine pages long. Written in plain English. Designed to be read and understood by every citizen — not just lawyers, not just professors, not just politicians.

When was the last time you read it?

I was taught the Constitution in elementary school in the 1960s. We read it. We discussed it. We understood what it said and what it meant. It was not presented as something mysterious or complex. It was presented as what it is: the operating manual for a self-governing republic.

Somewhere along the way, that changed. We were told the document was too complex for ordinary interpretation. That we needed experts. That constitutional questions belonged to specialists.

This is not an accident. A citizen who reads the Constitution understands something powerful: the document does not grant you rights. It restricts the government from violating rights you already possess. The Bill of Rights is not a gift from the government. It is a warning to the government.

That distinction changes everything. Because the moment you believe your rights come from the government, you accept that the government can take them back. And that is exactly what certain interests want you to believe.

But here's the part that keeps me up at night: freedom and responsibility are inseparable. The Constitution gives you the framework for an extraordinary life. But it demands something in return. It demands that you be a good citizen. That you participate. That you hold power accountable.

And we have stopped doing that.

300,000 local government seats in this country are sitting vacant or unchallenged right now. School boards. City councils. Planning commissions. Water districts. The positions that determine what your children are taught, what gets built in your neighborhood, and where your tax dollars go.

Nobody is filling them. Not because people don't care. Because people have been trained to believe that politics only happens in Washington. That their local government doesn't matter.

It matters more than anything happening in Washington. Because local government is where the Constitution actually touches your life.

Personal responsibility is not a slogan. It is the price of freedom. And right now, most of us are not paying it.
