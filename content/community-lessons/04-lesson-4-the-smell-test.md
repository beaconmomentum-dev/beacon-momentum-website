# LESSON 4: THE SMELL TEST

**How to write with AI without smelling like AI.**

---

We are living through the split. The platforms have divided your business into two halves. On the build side, they hand you the machine. On the trust side, they lock the door and demand a human.

Here is the problem. Ahrefs looked at roughly 900,000 new pages and found that 74% carried detectable AI text. Machine-written articles briefly crossed half of everything being published. But only about 14% of the articles that actually rank in Google are AI-generated. Half the volume. A sliver of the results.

And here is the detail that should change how you write: in a blind test, 56% of readers preferred the machine-written article, but 52% said they check out the moment they suspect a machine wrote it. They liked it better until they knew.

That is not a quality problem you can fix by paying for a better model. It is a suspicion problem. Suspicion is a craft problem. Craft can be taught.

The job is not to write better than the machine. The job is to not smell like one.

---

## The Six Tells

There is a real, specific, learnable list of things that set off the smell.

**1. The Three-Part Stack.** Not this. Not that. This. Three sentences in a row with the exact same shape. It reads like a drumbeat, and human beings don't talk in drumbeats. When you see this pattern, break it. Combine two, drop one, change the rhythm. Human conversation is messy. It is uneven. Perfect triplets signal a formula, not a person.

**2. The Mirror Sentence.** Two clauses of nearly identical length balanced perfectly against each other. It feels clever, and it is the single loudest tell there is. The machine will write, "While the cost of failure is high, the price of inaction is higher." A human will write, "Failing hurts, but doing nothing will kill you." The human version is unbalanced, direct, and real.

**3. Punchy Closers.** Every paragraph landing on a punchy little closer. Real people trail off. Real people put the good part in the middle and then keep talking about something adjacent. If every paragraph ends like a mic drop, your reader knows a machine wrote it. Let your paragraphs end naturally.

**4. Vocabulary Nobody Uses Out Loud.** Leverage as a verb. Unlock. Landscape. Ecosystem. Game-changing. Delve. If you wouldn't say it standing in a driveway talking to a neighbor, don't type it. These words take up space and sound important but carry no weight. Speak plainly.

**5. No Specifics with Edges.** Machines write around detail because they don't have any. A real number, a real day of the week, a real thing somebody said, a real thing that went wrong. That is what a machine can't fake and what a reader can't ignore. The machine will say, "Many businesses struggle with email marketing." A human will say, "We were losing a third of our subscribers before email four." The specific detail is the edge that cuts through.

**6. Nothing at Stake for the Writer.** No admission, no doubt, no place where you say the thing that costs you something to say. AI is programmed to be helpful and harmless. It won't take a risk. If your writing has no skin in the game, it has no pulse. When you admit a failure, you prove you are human. When you take a stand that might alienate someone, you prove you have a spine.

---

## The Seventh Tell: Perfect Paragraph Length

When every paragraph is exactly three to four lines long, a person didn't write it. Look at a real page of writing. Some paragraphs are one line. Some are dense blocks. That is what it looks like when somebody is thinking while typing. The machine writes in blocks because it is generating text to fill a space. A human writes in bursts and pauses because they are following a thought.

---

## The Before and After

| Before (Failed the Smell Test) | After (The Human Rewrite) |
| :--- | :--- |
| Our AI-powered email platform helps businesses unlock unprecedented growth. Not just automation. Not just personalization. Real results. Leverage cutting-edge technology to transform your customer engagement and drive measurable ROI across the entire customer lifecycle. | We built this because our own welcome sequence was losing about a third of new subscribers before email four and we couldn't figure out why. Turned out the timing was wrong, not the copy. The tool fixes the timing. It does about nine other things too but that's the one that got us to build it. |

The second version is shorter, has a number in it, admits a failure, and doesn't sell anything until the last line. It also has an odd rhythm, because that is what it sounds like when a person explains something.

---

## Exercise

Take your last published piece of content — an email, a blog post, a social update. Print it out. Hunt for these six tells by name.

Find the three-part stacks and break them. Find the mirror sentences and unbalance them. Kill the punchy closers. Cross out every word you wouldn't say out loud. Inject a specific detail with edges. Add something that costs you to say. Mess up your paragraph lengths.

Don't let the machine sit where the customer can feel it.
