### Tone Adjustments Recommended

**Adjustment 1: Strengthen the opening "I" voice.**

The blog post is currently written in third-person analytical voice ("There is an old form of fraud..."). The manifesto and the strongest Beacon content use first-person founder voice. For consistency with the manifesto launch, consider adding a one-line first-person frame at the top:

> *Current:* "There is an old form of fraud called check kiting."
>
> *Suggested:* "I want to show you something that should concern every person building a business on AI platforms right now. There is an old form of fraud called check kiting."

This is a minor adjustment. The analytical voice works for a Signal article — it reads more like journalism than a letter. If the intent is to publish this as a companion to the manifesto (which is deeply personal), the first-person frame connects the two. If the intent is to position it as a standalone research piece, the current voice is correct.

**Recommendation:** Keep as-is for the blog. The analytical voice differentiates Signal articles from the manifesto's personal voice. This creates two distinct registers — the manifesto is the founder speaking; the Signal is the research arm reporting.

**Adjustment 2: The "Build accordingly" close.**

This is strong. Two words. Imperative. No softening. It echoes the manifesto's demand for action. No change needed.

**Adjustment 3: The "Follow the Dollar" reference.**

The line "Beacon has been saying this since the Follow the Dollar Watch Brief" is the only moment where the piece sounds like it is selling something. It is brief and factual — Beacon did publish that Watch Brief — but it is the one line that breaks the journalistic register.

**Options:**
- Keep it (establishes continuity with existing Beacon content, rewards existing members who remember the Watch Brief)
- Remove it (makes the piece feel more like pure journalism, better for sharing with people who do not yet know Beacon)

**Recommendation:** Keep it. The manifesto already established that Beacon has been building. This line proves it with a specific prior publication. Existing members will recognize it. New readers will be curious about what else Beacon has published.

**Final verdict:** The blog post requires no substantive tone changes. It is ready to publish as written.

### "The AI Check Kite — What Builders Need to Understand"

There is an old form of fraud called check kiting. You write a check from an empty account, deposit it in another bank, write another check back before the first clears. Both accounts appear solvent. The moment one check clears before the next arrives, everything collapses.

On August 19, Forbes published two separate analyses arguing that $3 trillion in AI investment is structured with the same fundamental risk.

The numbers are staggering. $600 billion in on-balance-sheet infrastructure commitments from Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, and Microsoft. Another $2.4 trillion in off-balance-sheet purchase commitments and not-yet-started leases. Alphabet just reported its first quarter of negative free cash flow since going public. And the investment structure is circular — Company A invests in Company B, which uses that money to buy products from Company A. Nvidia and OpenAI sit at the center of this web.

This is not illegal. The technology is real. The productivity gains are documented. AI adoption is outpacing anything since the internet.

But the financial structure supporting it is fragile. And fragile structures break.

I am not writing this as a market analyst. I am writing this as someone who builds businesses and advises others who do the same. The lesson from the dot-com era is clear: when the bubble burst in 2000, the fiber-optic cables stayed in the ground. The internet kept working. The companies that built the cables went bankrupt. Every business wired exclusively to those companies had to scramble.

The same pattern is forming now. The AI infrastructure will survive. The question is which providers will be standing when the correction arrives — and whether your business is dependent on one of the ones that will not be.

The builder's framework is simple:

The tool was never the moat. The system you build around the tool is the moat. The sovereign infrastructure you own — diversified, not dependent on a single provider — is the moat. The ability to produce what your business needs without a single point of failure is the moat.

When the largest company in the world is burning cash faster than it generates it, the lesson for every founder, every operator, every builder is: do not confuse access to a tool with ownership of a system.

The music is still playing. Build as if it could stop tomorrow.
